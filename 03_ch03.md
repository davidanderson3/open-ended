# Chapter 3: The Skull on the Table
## Context
- Timeline position: Present day, early autumn, two days after the island
- POV: First-person narrator
- Location: Apartment kitchen / living room
- Key state (before): Guarded, curious, still refusing to call anyone
- Key state (after): More attached to the skull; unsettled by its domestic presence
- Continuity notes / open threads: Whether he will report it; how he will learn its story; why he keeps it close

I set the skull on the kitchen table the way you set down something fragile that might break your heart. I moved the mail aside, pushed the salt shaker to the corner, wiped the counter even though it wasn’t dirty. It felt important to make room for it, as if I could invent a ritual by being deliberate.

In daylight it looked smaller, less like a dare and more like a question. I turned off the ceiling light and pulled the lamp from the living room so the light would fall across it instead of straight down. I didn’t want it to be clinical. I wanted to be able to see it without the feeling that I was doing homework.

It was heavy in a way that didn’t match its size. I lifted it with both hands and felt the weight settle into my palms, the smooth crown warm already from the room. I knew the math of a skull, the general idea of mass and density, but what I felt was different. It had the quiet heft of a thing that had been handled before, held, set down, and then handled again. It felt like a cup that had passed through many hands.

I set it back down and let myself look. The missing jaw made the face look open, like it was forever about to speak. The eye sockets were deeper than I expected. When I peered inside the right one, I saw a glint of movement, no bigger than a comma. A spider, dark and compact, was tucked in the shadow. It startled me enough that I leaned back. It didn’t run. It waited.

I told myself it had been there on the island, and that I had carried it home along with everything else. It had come for the ride and was now the only living thing in the skull’s company. I watched it for a while. It didn’t flee or threaten. It simply shifted, its tiny legs adjusting to the bone as if the socket had always been a safe place. I didn’t want to evict it. It felt like a witness. It felt earned.

I ran my fingers along the ridge of the brow, the edge where skin would have met bone. I tried to picture a face, but my mind kept offering blankness, a shape without features. The skull was specific and anonymous at the same time. It had belonged to someone, which was obvious and somehow still difficult to accept. It was nobody now, which was worse.

On the island, the water had rinsed it clean. Here in my kitchen, it gathered a different kind of residue. The light of the lamp softened it. The smell of coffee drifted over it. The spider made the socket look occupied. The skull became part of the room, the way a new piece of furniture does when you have lived with it for a week: not yet familiar, already claimed.

I should have called someone. I should have done the reasonable thing. Instead I opened the fridge and looked for milk I didn’t need. I rinsed my mug twice. I thought about my brother and how he would have been decisive, loud about it, making a show of doing the right thing. I thought about how, in my life, I had practiced quiet decisions, the ones you can pretend you didn’t make.

I sat on the couch and looked at the skull across the room. The spider was still there, a small dark punctuation in the pale bone. It made me oddly calmer to know it wasn’t empty, that something lived, however briefly, in that hollow.

I kept telling myself I would take it to someone, that this was temporary. I said it out loud once, just to test how it sounded in the air. The skull did not answer. The spider moved deeper into the socket, as if to nest.

The afternoon stretched. The light changed. I made a list of phone numbers in my head and then crossed them off without dialing. The skull stayed where I had placed it, patient in the lamplight. I could pretend it was a teaching tool, a prop, a story that would eventually go back on the shelf.

But the way I kept glancing at it told me it had already taken its place.
